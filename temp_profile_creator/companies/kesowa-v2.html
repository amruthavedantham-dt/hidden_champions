Part 1

A rare, clean success in Kolkata — now colliding with a policy gap that could end the company

Kesowa and Federal Synergies (India) Private Limited are, in effect, the same operation running under two names. Every document behind Kesowa's flagship work — NKDA's tender, its Letter of Intent, its final completion certificate, and a CESC purchase order — names the contracting party as Federal Synergies (India) Private Limited (CIN U45400WB2008PTC129039), incorporated in 2008, eleven years before Kesowa itself, at the same Salt Lake, Kolkata address. Directors include Vrint and Prayush Poddar (common to both companies) plus two names not seen elsewhere in this research: Arjun Navada and Uday Kumar Ghose. The working theory — plausible, not independently confirmed — is that Federal Synergies is the real operating vehicle for large drone-survey contracts, while Kesowa Infinite Ventures houses the ARU software brand. If correct, this also explains why Kesowa's own filed revenue looked like an unexplained decline in an earlier pass: the flagship contract's revenue likely sat on a different entity's books entirely.

In February 2021, the company (contracting as Federal Synergies) began a four-year, roughly ₹3.88 crore drone-data contract with Newtown's civic authority, NKDA — and closed it exactly on schedule in May 2024, with zero penalty. Winning the work wasn't trivial: NKDA's December 2020 tender required bidders to show at least ₹5 crore in average turnover over three years, a real bar, and the Letter of Intent that followed confirmed acceptance at the full estimated value. NKDA's own Work Completion Certificate, issued February 2025, confirms both the dates and the clean outcome. A four-year municipal contract of this size closing on time and penalty-free is a genuinely rare thing to find independently documented.

Kesowa also has a real, paying private-sector client, not just government work. CESC Limited, the power utility serving Kolkata, placed an order in May 2022 for "Drone Based LT Network Surveillance" — 50 units at ₹25,100 each, ₹14,80,900 in total — a dated, named commercial contract, materially stronger evidence than the still-unconfirmed client logos flagged elsewhere in this research.

Since NKDA's Debasish Sen inaugurated it in April 2021, the platform behind this work — the Drone & Drone Data Call Centre — has run 2,442 missions and 1,233 mapping projects over three years, by the company's own count. Its actual public claim is being "India's first" drone call center — not "world's first"; no source anywhere, including a direct search for a global superlative, supports the broader claim, so it isn't used here.

The real story is what happened after a technically successful pilot. Kesowa doesn't want to be counted among India's 500-plus interchangeable drone-survey vendors, and its own account of the Newtown aftermath explains why. The engagement was conceived from the start as a pilot for a model, not an end in itself — but despite clean execution, the underlying data never became public. Only a handful of NKDA officers ever saw it, and when those officers moved to other postings, the momentum went with them. By the founder's own reckoning, of every ₹500 the contract spent, only around ₹100 in ongoing value was actually being generated — a return the company has judged isn't worth chasing further. It has since turned down additional one-off drone-survey offers rather than compete purely on price against venture-funded rivals bidding down government tenders.

The pivot itself isn't just talk. In February 2024, Kesowa wrote its own internal strategy memo — a "Drone & GIS Data Monetization Plan for NKDA" — laying out six concrete revenue use cases against data the city already owns: per-plot residential reports (₹2,000/report, roughly ₹1 crore/year at just 5,000 plots in one action area), commercial-plot reports with photo/video (₹30,000 per 5 years), high-resolution map-data resale to mobility/GIS companies (benchmarked against satellite imagery pricing, arguing drone data's 3cm resolution justifies roughly a 10x premium over 30cm satellite imagery), block-intelligence reports for banks and housing societies, solar-rooftop thermal inspection, and public map monetization. The memo predates the framing direction given for this profile — it's the company's own thinking, not something retrofitted to fit a narrative.

The policy gap Kesowa is pointing at is real, but narrower than it first sounds. India's National Geospatial Policy 2022 (notified 28 December 2022) genuinely replaced a prior security-clearance regime with self-certification for collecting geospatial data domestically — real, substantial liberalization of who can collect. But it doesn't require public release of city-scale data collected under a government contract, which is precisely the gap in the Newtown story: NKDA paid for four years of drone data collection, and the public — banks, developers, ordinary residents — never saw any of it. Google's own Photorealistic 3D Tiles product, for comparison, already covers 2,500+ cities across 49 countries; whether India is among them wasn't independently confirmed here. By the founder's own estimate, roughly 100 of the ~190-216 countries Google operates in have made city data public — his figure, not independently verified.

The company's current state is genuinely precarious. Kesowa has let go of its entire team, and by the founder's account he's currently running it alone. Real investor interest exists — Insight Partners, a named New York venture fund, reached out and held two conversations with him over the past year — but no funding has followed, specifically, in his telling, because India lacks the policy dialogue needed to support a recurring data-utility model over one-off contracting. Without that shift, he says the company is likely to close. A second real project was also named: a 2025 monsoon-season 3D drone survey of Aarey Colony, Mumbai, to help map the Mithi River's catchment area feeding Powai Lake — specific and plausible, though not independently corroborated here.

Part 2

Where the real gap sits: not drone surveys, but who gets to use city data once it exists

  • Elsewhere in the world, open, current, city-scale 3D data is already real infrastructure.

    Google's Photorealistic 3D Tiles product covers 2,500+ cities across 49 countries — a real, current, commercially available product category most Indian cities have no equivalent of. Whether India is among those 49 countries was not independently confirmed in this pass.

  • India's 2022 policy reform addressed who can collect data — not who can use it once collected.

    The National Geospatial Policy 2022 replaced prior security-clearance requirements with self-certification for domestic geospatial data collection — a genuine, substantial liberalization. It does not require that data collected under a government contract (like NKDA's) be made public. That's the specific, narrower gap the founder is describing, not a broader claim that India's policy hasn't moved at all.

  • By the founder's own account, roughly half the countries Google Maps operates in have made city data public — India hasn't.

    About 100 of the roughly 189-216 countries Google operates in, by his estimate from the call — not independently verified here, and presented as his claim rather than a confirmed statistic.

  • Kesowa has already sized specific use cases against one city's own data — not a hypothetical pitch.

    Its own February 2024 monetization plan for NKDA estimates ~₹1 crore/year from residential plot reports alone, at just 5,000 plots in a single action area — before any wider rollout. Six such use cases are laid out with explicit pricing logic, including a resale benchmark against satellite imagery pricing.

The addressable opportunity here isn't more drone-survey contracts — that market is real but commoditized, and Kesowa has deliberately walked away from it. The actual gap is a largely nonexistent market in India for recurring, public, monetizable city-scale geospatial data — something NKDA has already funded the underlying capture for, once, without ever converting it into ongoing public value. Whether banks, developers, and citizens would actually pay for it at the prices Kesowa has sketched is untested beyond one internal memo; whether government will change the contracting model at all is, on the founder's own account, the open question the company's survival now depends on.

Part 3

Constraints, read as opportunities

Four places worth a closer look, each built on something Kesowa has already proven or already written down.

Velocity

Turn the NKDA completion certificate into an active reference, not a filed document

A clean, on-schedule, zero-penalty, government-certified 4-year contract closure is genuinely rare — and, by the founder's own account, almost nobody outside a handful of NKDA officers has ever seen the underlying data or even the fact that it closed this cleanly. Publicizing the completion certificate itself, not just the pitch, is a concrete, low-cost first step.

NKDA Work Completion Certificate; founder interview.

Margin

Replicate the CESC model with other private utilities

Power, water, and telecom utilities all have similar recurring "linear infrastructure inspection" needs to CESC's LT-network survey. That's a proven, non-government commercial contract type already executed once — a faster path to revenue than re-litigating the government utility-model argument from zero.

CESC Limited Purchase Order.

Margin

Formalize the Federal Synergies / Kesowa entity split for investor materials

Right now, an outside investor (like Insight Partners) evaluating "Kesowa" would need to understand that its flagship contract, revenue, and financial track record actually sit with a different, older legal entity. Making that structure explicit and clean — rather than something a researcher has to reverse-engineer from tender documents — removes a real diligence friction point.

ZaubaCorp; NKDA/CESC documents.

Velocity

Pilot the NKDA monetization plan's use cases in one additional city

The entire "data utility, not contractor" pitch currently rests on a single city's pilot. Executing even one use case from the February 2024 monetization memo — say, resident plot reports — in a second city would be a materially stronger proof point for both government and investor conversations than the Newtown case study alone.

Kesowa did the hard part. It ran a real, four-year, ₹3.88 crore government drone-data contract to a clean, on-schedule, zero-penalty close — the kind of outcome most companies profiled in this series never get independently certified in writing. It is now at genuine risk of closing anyway — not because the work failed, but because the founder has chosen not to keep doing more of it on the terms available, betting instead that India will start treating city data as a recurring public utility rather than a one-off government expense. Real investors have listened twice. No one has funded the bet yet.
