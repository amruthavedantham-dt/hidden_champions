Part 1

A real but very small operation, with one substantiated flagship project and a steep recent revenue drop

Kesowa began as Kesowa Infinite Ventures LLP (LLPIN AAP-9584), founded 17 July 2019 in Kolkata by three co-founders — Prayush Poddar (CEO & CMO), Vrint Poddar (CTO), and Vinayak Malhotra. That LLP converted to the current entity, Kesowa Infinite Ventures Private Limited (CIN U31906WB2020PTC240808), on 24 October 2020. Vinayak Malhotra does not appear as a director of the successor company, and his official director record (DIN 08512755) now shows only an unrelated current directorship — consistent with him stepping back around the conversion rather than continuing with the business.

Revenue was ₹46.72 lakh in FY2025, down 39% year-on-year — a steep drop, not a minor softening. No cause was independently confirmed despite a direct search; this is a first-party question (e.g. whether a specific government project completed without a replacement contract yet lined up) that only the founders can really answer. The company runs on a very small base: ~14-16 employees, ₹5 lakh paid-up capital, recognized as a DPIIT (Startup India) startup. It has drawn investment from the Sardar Patel Technology Business Incubator (a Mumbai-based accelerator with 89 portfolio investments), though the specific amount and date weren't independently findable.

Kesowa's actual business is a drone-data SaaS/analytics layer, not drone manufacturing — MCA classifies its activity as "IT consulting and support services." Its two main products are the Drone Call Center (an on-demand aerial-intelligence platform, claiming "20x higher resolution" imagery) and ARU Skynet/ARU Server (drone-data management software with AI features including a Tree Count AI model and a Visual Pollution Index). These technical claims are self-published, with no independent benchmark or patent filing found to substantiate them. Whether Kesowa itself operates/pilots drones, or works entirely through third-party pilots processing imagery through its software, was not established despite a direct search.

The most credible evidence in this profile is a specific, named government/PSU project. Kesowa completed a drone survey for Eastern Coalfields Limited (ECL), a real Coal India subsidiary, assessing haul-road and slope safety at its open-cast mine in Kalipahari, West Bengal, after the site had experienced heavy rain — producing a 3D model in place of a slower, more dangerous manual survey. That level of specific, checkable detail (named subsidiary, named location, a specific triggering event) is meaningfully stronger than a bare client-logo claim, even though it's still Kesowa's own published case study rather than independent press. Separately, an independent press source corroborates real project work with Kolkata Municipal Corporation and the West Bengal Pollution Control Board specifically. The remaining nine names on Kesowa's client list (including NKDA, Maharashtra Labour Welfare Board, Tamil Nadu, Nagpur Municipal Corporation, CESC, and others) are self-claimed only.

Part 2

A fast-growing, policy-supported market — dominated in the public narrative by hardware makers Kesowa isn't one of

  • India's drone market is growing at a healthy pace, with real government policy behind it.

    $1.32 billion (2025) heading to $2.73 billion by 2034, an 8.45% CAGR (one source; a second puts 2024 at $1.58B — broadly consistent magnitude). A proposed PLI 2.0 scheme (~₹1,000 crore) targets 30% local drone production, on top of an existing ₹120 crore PLI allocation.

  • But the market's loudest names are playing a different game entirely.

    ideaForge, Garuda Aerospace, and Asteria Aerospace — the most-cited players — are drone HARDWARE manufacturers (ideaForge alone won ~₹100 crore in additional defense procurement in a single month, Nov 2025). Kesowa doesn't build drones; it builds software that analyzes what drones capture. These aren't real competitors so much as a different business entirely wearing the same industry label.

  • The closer comparison is a company most market write-ups don't lead with.

    Skylark Drones — a drone-data-and-analytics services company, not a hardware maker — is the more meaningful peer for Kesowa's actual business model, even though it isn't independently sized here.

  • Government and PSU procurement is a real, if narrow, demand channel — and it's the one Kesowa has actually broken into.

    Municipal corporations, pollution control boards, and coal-mining PSUs represent a recurring, tender-driven demand source distinct from the defense/consumer segments the bigger hardware names chase.

Kesowa is a genuinely tiny player — 14-16 people, under ₹50 lakh in revenue — inside a market whose public narrative is dominated by much larger hardware companies it doesn't actually compete with. Its real foothold is narrower and more specific: a small number of real government and PSU relationships, won project by project, in a market segment (drone-data services, not drone manufacturing) that gets less attention than the hardware story but is arguably the more honest description of what Kesowa actually does.

Part 3

Constraints, read as opportunities

Four places worth a closer look, each built on something Kesowa already has or a gap already visible in its own numbers.

Margin

Diagnose the 39% revenue drop directly

A drop this steep isn't self-explanatory. Pinning down whether a completed government project simply hasn't been replaced yet, or whether something more structural is at play, is the single most urgent open question here.

Velocity

Turn the ECL project into independently verifiable proof

The Eastern Coalfields case study is specific and credible, but it's still self-published. A named testimonial, a joint press mention, or an ECL/Coal India acknowledgment would convert a strong internal case study into external proof the next government tender committee can independently check.

Margin

Substantiate the technical claims

"20x higher resolution" and the Tree Count AI / Visual Pollution Index models are currently marketing language with no independent benchmark or patent behind them. Even a basic published accuracy metric, or a first patent filing, would give these claims real weight in a competitive tender.

Velocity

Clarify and formalize the drone-operations model

Whether Kesowa flies its own drones or works through partner pilots wasn't established here, and it's not a small detail — it changes the pitch, the regulatory footprint (DGCA relevance), and how scalable the business actually is. Settling this internally, then stating it plainly to clients, removes an ambiguity a careful government buyer would eventually ask about anyway.
